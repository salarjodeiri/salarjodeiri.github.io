Product and Operations

I build products and the operating systems behind them.

I work where product meets operations. I decide what gets built from evidence, then build the supplier standards, quality routines, and internal tools that let a team deliver it reliably. For more than four years I owned the roadmap and portfolio of a consumer product range and ran the operation behind it: an external network of 20+ print shops and binding workshops, quality control, inventory, the webshop and marketplace channels, and the internal tooling the range ran on.

Most recently I was Product Owner of a data product with an in-product AI support agent, taken from concept to live release in Scrum, while working across six other product teams toward one shared showcase. Each case below covers the problem, the approach, the decisions and trade-offs, and the outcome.

Case 01 / Product and operations

BON: a product range, the operation behind it, and the tools that connected them

Over more than four years I owned the roadmap and portfolio of a premium stationery range, ran the outsourced production network that made it, and built the internal tooling that connected sales to production. The three chapters below are one system: portfolio decisions set what the workshops made, and the tooling made both faster.

Chapter A

Portfolio strategy: running a range as a portfolio, not a collection

Gave every line a job, let business cases and small pilots decide what got made, and grew the channels that carried it, from a direct webshop to 10+ marketplaces.

Problem

BON made premium notebooks, planners, and sketchbooks for a market with an extreme seasonal peak, produced through an external network of print shops and binding workshops rather than owned production. Dated planners had weeks, not months, to sell before they lost their value, so a wrong call on what to make, how much, and when showed up immediately as unsold stock. The range had grown faster than the evidence behind it. The problem was not demand. It was that too little in the range was decided from evidence, and too few products had a clear reason to exist.

Approach

Every line got a job. I set a stated purpose for each product line: one to drive purchases, one to expand presence in stores, one for reach, one for awareness in video and marketing, one for the gift market, and one to show the design spirit of the brand. That turned range reviews from a debate about taste into a question each product could be measured against.

Investment followed evidence. I built roughly 20 to 30 product business cases a year in Excel, covering cost, price, margin, and positioning, informed by customer research, sales team reports, and recent best-seller data on colors and models. About half were approved for production. Before a full run, I tested demand with customer polls, A/B concept tests, and small pilots. I ran dashboards on best sellers, customer retention, and product specifications, and used them with inventory turnover to set reorder timing and production volumes across the range. Priorities across functions were set in a standing weekly cross-functional planning session.

Collaborations as a growth lever. I worked with 30+ artists, ran 15+ exhibitions with artists as partners, and co-branded products with 15+ brands. Each season I refreshed cover materials and colors across 150+ SKUs and shipped two to three limited or special editions, created for and with artists and designers.

Channels and platforms. I launched the direct webshop from zero, owning catalog structure, product data, and the online range, and set up and ran the listings across 10+ third-party marketplace platforms, including Digikala. I selected and rolled out the ERP and inventory system and the e-commerce platform the business ran on, and set up user accounts, roles, and access permissions across them.

Key decisions and trade-offs

Cut before adding

After a portfolio review on per-product performance, I cut about 40% of the catalog rather than launch into a crowded range. It meant retiring products with loyal buyers, but it freed attention and working capital for the models that earned them.

Say no to half the cases

Roughly half of each year's business cases did not reach production. A high rejection rate was the point: the cases that failed did so on paper, not in the warehouse.

Small runs, fast signal

Limited editions ran at roughly 100 to 300 units and usually sold out within a month. Keeping runs small protected cash and kept the range fresh; together, collaborations and limited editions reached roughly 10 to 15% of sales.

Ship inside the season

Late in a peak season the range needed a planner, a product I had never designed. I learned how and shipped it inside that same season, rather than wait a year for the next window.

Outcome

Annual product volume grew from roughly 500 to more than 30,000 units. The webshop grew from zero to more than 9,000 orders and 7,000 customers, alongside 35+ premium retail partners, 400+ B2B clients, 10+ marketplace channels, and an export line, all carrying one consistent range. Refreshed colors sold roughly twice as fast as older ones, and about half of each year's business cases were approved for production, so the range grew on proven demand rather than on volume of ideas.

Chapter B

Operations and supply: an outsourced network run to one standard

Graded and grew a network of 20+ print shops and binding workshops, set up daily quality inspection and staged supplier onboarding, and planned capacity a year ahead of peak.

Problem

Every BON product was made outside the company, by print shops and binding workshops of very different capability. Quality and delivery depended on which workshop a job happened to land with, and demand concentrated in one short peak season, so a late or rejected batch could not be recovered by waiting. Growth made this worse: each new workshop added capacity and added risk at the same time. The range needed an operation that held one standard across partners the company did not control.

Approach

Grade, then allocate. I graded every workshop from A to D on capability and reliability and allocated production against that grading. Workshops were downgraded on late response and missed deadlines, so the grade stayed a live signal rather than a label.

Earn volume, do not assume it. I built a staged onboarding pipeline: reference checks with each workshop's existing customers, a six-month trial on current products, then responsibility increased step by step. New workshops joined the network as volume grew, each through the same gate.

Check quality where it is made. I set up a standing process with two staff visiting the workshops, checking output daily against specification, and reporting back to the team. Delivery and quality performance was tracked in one shared sheet, and my direct team reviewed the previous day's performance and the day's issues at a board each day.

Capacity before demand. I planned production capacity a year ahead of peak season, preparing workshops by supplying material, supporting hiring, and completing machinery, and kept substitute workshops ready. I issued and tracked purchase and production orders across the network and material suppliers, negotiated lead times, and flagged risks such as material scarcity early in monthly production reporting.

Inventory and fulfillment. I managed third-party warehouse providers on service level, ran regular stock counts and reconciled recorded against physical stock, and used reorder data to set production volumes.

Key decisions and trade-offs

Make versus buy

I produced the full range through the external network instead of investing in owned capacity. I accepted higher per-unit cost and offset it through specification control and faster stock turn.

Slow trust on purpose

A workshop typically reached trusted status only after about two and a half years. It slowed onboarding, but it kept specification under control across partners the company did not own.

Own the failure with the client

When a newly onboarded workshop could not deliver a peak-season order, I handled it directly with the client: explained the cause, compensated with the best products available, and delivered later, with adjustments outside normal practice.

Outcome

Over the period, on-time delivery rose from 70% to 85%, the reject rate fell from 20% to 10%, and the return rate from 5% to 2%. Stock cycle time, the time stock sat in the warehouse before selling through, fell from six months to under two for 70% of the range, freeing working capital tied up in slow-moving stock.

Chapter C

Internal tooling: a cost and quoting app that took sales out of the production queue

Built with Claude Code in the final phase at BON, for the sales and production teams, so a custom B2B request could be priced on the spot.

Problem

BON served 400+ B2B clients, and almost every custom request differed in materials, finishing, and quantity. Pricing one meant combining costs across printing, binding, gilding, pre-press and post-press, paper, and materials, all of which moved with the market. Sales could not answer a client without routing the request back to production, so each quote waited in someone else's queue for 3 to 5 days, and the team completed only 1 to 2 custom requests a week.

Approach

I treated the handoff as the product problem. The users were colleagues, and the job to be done was "give a client a reliable price without asking production." I built a live production cost app with Claude Code, fed by input costs from the production department across every process step and material, and kept it current through recurring messages to suppliers. I worked with the production and sales teams to keep the cost structure aligned with how each function actually worked, then designed the internal sales and production workflows around the app, also built with Claude Code, to replace the manual back-and-forth between the two teams.

Key decisions

Put the answer where the question arrives

The tool sat with sales, not production. The goal was not a better spreadsheet for the cost owner; it was removing the round trip for the person facing the client.

Current beats precise

Input prices moved constantly, so keeping the inputs fresh through a supplier routine mattered more than modeling every edge case. A slightly simpler model that was always current was trusted; a perfect one that was stale would not have been.

AI as the build tool

I used Claude Code to build and iterate quickly with the teams using it. The product is an internal tool, not an AI feature; AI shortened the path from problem to working software.

Outcome

Custom B2B quote turnaround fell from 3 to 5 days to 1 to 2 days, and to as little as four hours in some cases. Custom requests completed each week rose from 1 to 2 to 6 to 7, roughly tripling throughput on that workflow without adding people.

Case 02 / Data product and AI

Wardrobe Mirror: a data product from board game to live release

Took a research idea to a live web app with an in-product AI support agent across five sprints, lifted task completion from 54% to 78%, and collected a clean dataset that produced usable findings.

Role
Product Owner
Timeframe
Feb to Jul 2026, five two-week sprints
Team
6-person cross-functional team, plus alignment with six other product teams
Context
Applied digital transformation program with an institutional client and partner institutions

Problem

How a garment is actually worn, valued, and let go is poorly understood, yet the use phase drives most of fashion's environmental impact and sits at the center of emerging EU Digital Product Passport requirements. Existing research either scaled but ignored perceived value, or captured value but not behavior. The brief was to build an instrument that makes the gap between what people say and what they do observable for a single garment, and that people would actually finish.

Approach

I framed it as a product, not a survey. Each participant reflects on three real garments (a recent purchase, a favorite, and one they are ready to let go), sampling the wardrobe from entry to exit in one short session, and gets a personal profile in return. That dual value, something useful for the user and clean data for the client, shaped every decision.

I ran delivery in Scrum and took the product through six iterations, from a physical board game and decision tree to a standalone web app on a live database. I owned the backlog end to end on Kanban boards in Trello and Notion, breaking the roadmap into epics and user stories with acceptance criteria and refining them each sprint with developers and designers. I partnered with engineering and UX on the data model, integrated a Google Sheets data source through an API, and designed the product logic: a scoring engine with four behavioral value indicators and a six-archetype classifier that returns a personalized profile while producing a structured research dataset from the same interaction. We built the working app as a team in Figma and Claude Code, and I set up a scheduled daily check and repair routine on the live app to replace manual monitoring.

AI inside the product. I built the AI support agent: I wrote and maintained its knowledge base, designed its conversation flows and the point at which it handed off to a person, and tracked how often it resolved a question without one. We also launched a version that generated each question from the participant's previous answer.

Key decisions and trade-offs

Completion over completeness

Version 5 added questions that pushed completion time past seven minutes and caused drop-off. I read that as a measured regression, cut it back in version 6, and kept the highest-value additions optional. Finishing the session mattered more than collecting every field.

Kept the trade-off visible

I kept the three sections in lifecycle order for coherence, after weighing a disposal-first order that would have protected the most valuable answers from drop-off. I documented the trade-off for the client rather than hiding it.

Partial data is still data

Participants can stop after any section. Rather than discard partial sessions, I kept them with a lower confidence tier, which protected sample size without overstating certainty.

Working across teams

Six other teams in the program, each with its own Product Owner, were building products in mental health, journalism, the history of education, and legal tech. I worked across those teams to align how each would present on showcase day and to keep every team at a comparable stage through the five sprints. The teams presented to each other to critique each other's products, ran tips and tops, and ran workshops so each team understood the others' work.

Outcome

Completion through all three sections rose from 54% to 78% across rounds, and median completion time fell from over seven minutes to under five. The product collected 500+ garment records from 148 participants, and statistical testing surfaced concrete findings, including a median value-per-use of 0.95 euro per wear and the result that purchase price did not predict how often a garment was worn. I co-authored the research paper and presented the product at the closing showcase.

Case 03 / Product systems

Biloba: a classification system that let a garden center scale its expertise

Turned scattered, person-dependent product knowledge into a documented system that let 100+ staff explain 500+ products consistently, and cut new-hire onboarding to a half-day.

Role
Product Strategy Consultant, part-time
Timeframe
Jan to Jun 2025
Team
Led a two-person writer and designer team
Context
A 100+-staff garden center moving upmarket toward B2B

Problem

Biloba had grown organically for years into a large garden center, but its product information and identity had not kept up. Positioning was unclear, and 100+ staff (15+ in sales) struggled to categorize and explain hundreds of plants and consumables to customers. The owner wanted to move upmarket and win B2B work from architects and designers, which needed one consistent system across every touchpoint.

Approach

I ran discovery through 10 interviews with the owner over five months, plus competitor and field research, and defined the mission, five customer personas across two segments (end customers and professionals), positioning, and value proposition. From there I designed a product classification and naming system: size, light, water, care, and pet-safety pictograms, packaging categories, a per-plant product guide covering 500+ plants, and names for 8 garden spaces. I documented it in a 36-page reference for current and future team members.

Outcome

New-hire sales onboarding dropped to a half-day of structured reading, and every new hire now starts with the same product knowledge. Within a year of rollout, client numbers grew 20%, including new architecture studios as B2B clients, and in-garden engagement rose 15% over six months.

Case 04 / Product discovery and development

Neshiman: reshaping a product range around how it is used

Turned the showroom into a research source, took 15+ seating models from prototype into production, and gave the sales team consistent product information, lifting product sales 10 to 15% within a year.

Role
Product Developer
Timeframe
2019 to 2021
Team
Partnered with furniture designers and craft workshops
Context
Designer furniture maker, 200+ staff, 5 stores

Problem

Neshiman produced around 75 to 80 base models across roughly 15 categories, with more than half made by external craftsmen. The range had expanded faster than the company's read on how customers actually used and valued each piece. The feedback loop from the showroom floor back into the range was informal, and the sales team lacked consistent information to explain what set each piece apart.

Approach

I ran user interviews and spent time on the showroom floor talking with customers about their experience with the products and the brand, and paired that with research into materials, color, form, and use. Rather than only add new models, I remodeled existing base models around what I learned, for example reworking the Daneshgah chair, and built and tested prototypes with designers and craftsmen before anything went to production. In parallel I sourced new workshops to widen production options, and built product specification data and guidelines so the sales team could describe the collection consistently and point customers to the right piece.

Outcome

I took 15+ seating models across sofas, chairs, and stools from prototype into production, and the product changes increased product sales 10 to 15% within one year. The specification data and guidelines reached about 25 salespeople across 5 stores, giving new and existing staff the same product knowledge.

How I work

Principles, each with the evidence behind it

Every product needs a job

At BON each line had one stated purpose, so reviews measured products against it instead of against opinion.

Let the evidence say no

About half of 20 to 30 yearly business cases were not approved, and about 40% of the catalog was cut after review.

Protect the outcome metric, not the scope

At Wardrobe Mirror I rolled back a version that added value on paper but cost completion in practice.

When the process is the bottleneck, build the tool

The cost and quoting app removed a handoff that no amount of process discipline would have fixed.

Align teams around a shared standard

A weekly cross-functional planning session at BON; shared showcase standards across seven product teams at Wardrobe Mirror.

Standards before scale

Every workshop was graded A to D and new ones earned volume through a six-month trial, so growth did not outrun quality.

Plan capacity before demand

Workshops were prepared a year ahead of peak, with material, hiring support, and substitutes in place before orders arrived.

Build something that runs without me

After the handover, the team delivered the next peak season at over 85% on time.

Case 05 / Foundation

Architecture and urban design

Before product, I practiced architecture and urban design, delivering two major projects from master planning through detailed technical documentation: a 100+ unit BIM-based residential development (Revit and AutoCAD) and a large public cultural garden. I coordinated client requirements with architecture and engineering teams, prepared drawings for planning approval, and worked on site with the contractors building the work. This is where I learned to hold a whole system and its finest details at once, and to turn ambiguous constraints into documentation other people could execute from. Product work runs on the same habits.
