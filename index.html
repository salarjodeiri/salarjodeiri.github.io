Case 01 / Data product

Wardrobe Mirror: a data product from board game to live release

Took a research idea to a live web app across five sprints, and lifted task completion from 54% to 78% while collecting a clean dataset that produced usable findings.

Role
Product Manager, Data Products
Timeframe
Feb to Jul 2026, 6 iterations
Team
6-person cross-functional team (data, engineering, UX, fashion, education)
Context
Applied program with an institutional client and partner institutions

Problem

How a garment is actually worn, valued, and let go is poorly understood, yet the use phase drives most of fashion's environmental impact and sits at the center of new EU rules on product data. Existing research either scaled but ignored perceived value, or captured value but not behavior. The brief was to build an instrument that makes the gap between what people say and what they do observable for a single garment, and that people would actually finish.

Approach

I framed it as a product, not a survey. Each participant reflects on three real garments (a recent purchase, a favorite, and one they are ready to let go), sampling the wardrobe from entry to exit in one short session, and gets a personal profile in return. That dual value, something useful for the participant and clean data for the research, shaped every decision.

I ran delivery in Scrum across five sprints and took the instrument through six iterations, from a physical board game and decision tree to a standalone web app on a live database. I partnered with engineering and UX to define the data model and integrate a data source through an API, and I designed the product logic: a scoring engine with four behavioral value indicators and a six-archetype classifier that returns a personalized profile while producing a structured research dataset from the same interaction.

Key decisions

Completion over completeness

Version 5 added questions that pushed completion time past seven minutes and caused drop-off. I read that as a measured regression, cut it back in version 6, and kept the highest-value additions optional. Finishing the session mattered more than collecting every field.

Kept the trade-off visible

I kept the three sections in lifecycle order for coherence, after weighing a disposal-first order that would have protected the most valuable answers from drop-off. I documented the trade-off rather than hiding it.

Partial data is still data

Participants can stop after any section. Rather than discard partial sessions, I kept them with a lower confidence tier, which protected sample size without overstating certainty.

Outcome

Completion through all three sections rose from 54% to 78% across rounds, and median completion time fell from over seven minutes to under five. The instrument collected 500+ garment records from 148 participants, and statistical testing surfaced concrete findings, including a median value-per-use of 0.95 euro per wear and the result that purchase price did not predict how often a garment was worn. I co-authored the research paper and presented the product at the closing showcase.

Case 02 / Consumer product

BON: building and scaling a design-led product portfolio

Turned a seasonal, unpredictable product business into a repeatable discovery-to-launch engine, scaling annual volume from about 500 to more than 30,000 units.

Role
Product Manager, full lifecycle
Timeframe
2018 to Jan 2026
Team
Grew and led a team from 2 to 12 across product, design, sales, operations
Context
Premium stationery, extreme seasonal demand, time-sensitive stock

Problem

BON sold premium notebooks and planners into a market with an extreme seasonal peak. Early on the business could not prepare for that peak: it lost orders and customers, tied up cash in slow stock, and ran on guesswork. Dated planners had weeks, not months, to sell before they lost their value. The problem was not demand, it was the absence of a repeatable system to decide what to make, how much, and when.

Approach

I built a discovery habit. Before each season I tested demand with Instagram story polls and SMS campaigns, then designed products around what customers actually asked for. I validated before committing: small pilot batches of around 1,000 units and A/B concept tests (for example a tracker versus an A5 planner, and cover variants), marketed both, and kept only what sold.

I rationalized the portfolio, cutting the range by about 40% from roughly 30 models to concentrate capital on the best sellers, while keeping a small long tail for B2B optionality. Across 15 to 20 core models and about 180 SKUs I engineered deliberate non-overlap on use, paper weight, and binding. On the supply side I stayed asset-light, partnering with the best print and binding workshops rather than buying machines, pre-printing three months ahead to beat the seasonal bottleneck, and pre-selling to retail to de-risk time-sensitive stock.

Key decisions

Make versus buy

I stayed asset-light to protect speed and cash in a volatile environment, accepting higher per-unit cost and less direct control, and offset it by targeting the premium segment where quality, not price, wins.

Cut breadth for shelf life

I cut about 40% of the catalog even though each model had some demand, because capital efficiency and shelf life mattered more than range. Shelf life for 70% of the portfolio fell from six months to under two.

Validate small, then scale

Pilots and pre-sales de-risked every launch, so investment followed evidence of demand rather than preceding it.

Outcome

Annual volume grew from about 500 to more than 30,000 units and the team from 2 to 12. I launched the e-commerce channel from zero to more than 9,000 orders and 7,000 customers, expanded to 35+ premium retail partners and 400+ B2B clients, and opened a UK export line. The business moved from operating at a loss to sustained profitability.

Case 03 / Product strategy

Biloba: a classification system that let a garden center scale its expertise

Turned an organically grown business into a coherent, premium brand and built a naming system that let 100+ staff explain 500+ products consistently.

Role
Product Strategy Consultant, part-time
Timeframe
Jan to Jun 2025
Team
Led a two-person writer and designer team
Context
A 100+-staff garden center moving upmarket toward B2B

Problem

Biloba had grown organically for years into a large garden center, but its identity and product information had not kept up. Positioning was unclear, the visual and verbal identity were inconsistent, and 100+ staff (15+ in sales) struggled to categorise and explain thousands of plants and consumables to customers. The owner wanted to move upmarket and win B2B work from architects and designers, which needed a studio-level, consistent identity across every touchpoint.

Approach

I ran discovery through 10 interviews with the owner over five months, plus competitor and field research, and defined the mission, five customer personas across two segments (end customers and professionals), positioning, and value proposition. From there I designed a product classification and naming system: size, light, water, care, and pet-safety pictograms, packaging categories, a per-plant product guide covering 500+ plants, and names for 8 garden spaces. I brought it together as a single reference for current and future team members, delivered with a two-person team.

Outcome

New-hire sales onboarding dropped to a half-day of structured reading, and every new hire now starts with the same product knowledge. After rollout, client numbers grew 20% within a year, including new architecture studios, and in-garden customer engagement rose 15% over six months. The classification system turned scattered, person-dependent knowledge into a repeatable product the whole team could use.

Case 04 / Foundation

Architecture and urban design

Before product, I practiced architecture and urban design, delivering two major projects: a 100+ unit BIM-based residential development (Revit and AutoCAD) and a large public garden on the Abbas-Abad cultural lands in central Tehran, both from master planning to detailed design. This is where I learned to hold a whole system and its finest details at once, coordinate work across disciplines, and turn ambiguous constraints into decision-ready plans. Those are the same habits product work runs on, which is why the move into product felt like a continuation rather than a change of direction.
